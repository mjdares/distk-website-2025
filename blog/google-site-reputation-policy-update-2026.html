Google's Site Reputation Policy Update: What Changed in August 2026

Google did not loosen its rules on placing third-party content on trusted domains. It published the factors its reviewers use and split enforcement by geography. Here is what that means for publishers and agencies, especially outside Europe.

Distk Editorial Oct 2026 12 min read

Google's site reputation policy targets third-party content published on a host site mainly because of that host's established ranking signals. On 28 August 2026, following discussion with the European Commission, Google changed enforcement: from 30 August 2026, a site reputation manual action directly affects results for the affected section for users outside the EEA, while inside the EEA its impact does not apply and the section may instead rank independently. For searchers in India, effects are unchanged. Google also published four review factors: presentation, quality, authorship and duplication, none of which is decisive alone. Syndication, user-generated content, editorial work, disclosed advertorials and appropriately treated affiliate links are explicitly not violations. Affected sites can submit reconsideration requests, and eligible sites can now pursue mediation.

What Is Google's Site Reputation Policy in 2026?

The site reputation policy is a Google spam policy against publishing third-party content on a trusted website mainly to exploit that site's ranking signals. Google's spam policies state it applies "where third-party content is published on a host site mainly because of that host's already-established ranking signals, which it has earned primarily from its first-party content," with the goal of ranking better than the content could on its own. Google introduced it in 2024 and updated its enforcement on 28 August 2026.

The 2026 update matters for two groups. Anyone buying or selling placements on established domains needs the current wording, not the 2024 version. And anyone whose audience or site touches the European Economic Area needs to understand that, from 30 August 2026, the same manual action has different effects inside and outside the EEA.

What Counts as Third-Party Content Under the Policy in 2026?

Google defines third-party content as "content that's created by an entity that's separate from the established host site." Its examples of separate entities are users of that site, freelancers, white-label services, and content created by people not employed directly by the host site. Critically, Google states that having third-party content alone is not a violation; it is only inconsistent with the policy "if the third-party content is published on a host site mainly because of that host site's already-established ranking signals."

Examples Google says are inconsistent with the policy

  • "An educational site hosting a page about sponsored reviews of payday loans written by a third-party that distributes the same page to other sites across the web."
  • "A medical site hosting a low-quality, third-party advertising page about 'best casinos' that isn't integrated with the site, and that's being placed on the site to rank better due to the established site's ranking signals."

Examples Google says are NOT inconsistent with the policy

  • Wire service or press release service sites.
  • News publications that have syndicated news content from other news publications.
  • Sites designed to allow user-generated content, such as a forum website or comment sections.
  • Columns, opinion pieces, articles, and other work of an editorial nature.
  • Third-party content such as "advertorial" or "native advertising" pages "where the purpose is to share content directly to readers (such as through promotion within the publication itself), rather than hosting the content to manipulate search rankings."
  • "Using affiliate links throughout a page, with links treated appropriately, or embedding third-party ad units throughout a page."

Google notes these lists are non-exhaustive.

How Did Enforcement Change in August 2026?

The policy's rules did not change; the consequences of a manual action now depend on where the searcher is. Google's announcement says that from 30 August, "manual actions applied under our site reputation policy will have a different effect for those searching in the EEA than outside of it." Google also states it is clarifying the criteria it considers when applying the policy.

Searcher locationEffect of a site reputation manual action from 30 August 2026
Outside the EEA, including IndiaThe manual action "will directly affect search results for the portion of the site affected." As before, the rest of the site is not affected.
Inside the EEA"The impact of the manual action won't apply. The affected section of the site may be separated in our systems so that, over time, it ranks independently from the rest of the site."

Google adds that because many pages are viewed globally, a page might carry a manual action that only affects results shown to users outside the EEA. Its policy FAQ confirms a manual action outside the EEA is not used as a ranking signal within the EEA, that there is no obligation to noindex affected content, and that previous EEA manual actions under this policy are lifted.

For an Indian business, the plain reading is that nothing softened. Searchers in India are outside the EEA, so a site reputation manual action affects results in India exactly as before.

How Does Google Decide Whether Content Is Integrated in 2026?

Through human review, and Google has now published the factors. Its spam policies say Google generally presumes individual pages match the overall quality of the domain, and if a portion of a site may be out of line with the policy, it "will be subject to human review." The goal is to decide whether content is "created with sufficient input, editorial oversight, or contribution from the host site to be considered fully integrated with the main site."

Factor Google listsThe question Google asks
Presentation"Are the graphic design, formatting, typography and UX features of the content consistent with the host domain?"
Quality"Are there quality issues present on the page that don't appear on the main domain and otherwise suggest a departure from the standard of quality of the main domain?"
Authorship"Is there an explicit acknowledgement of ownership or responsibility for the content? Are there indications that contest the stated authorship of the content?"
DuplicationDoes "the same identical or near-identical content" appear on multiple other sites?

Google stresses that "not one of these factors is either necessary or sufficient on its own," and that other evidence may be considered, for example if pages are part of third-party marketing material for manipulating Search ranking.

Google's worked examples

  • Not likely to take action: a publisher's coupons and deals section, run with a specialist partner on a different CMS but fully integrated, clearly disclosed as commercial, curated, and with the publisher identified as editorially responsible.
  • Likely to take action: an unauthored affiliate article on a business publication linking to a CBD marketplace, with no identified author or editor, no commercial disclaimer, no links from the site's sections, and content duplicating a third-party marketplace.
  • Unlikely to take action: a news site's cooking section with affiliate links, produced by an identified freelancer, under clear editorial responsibility, with content unique to the host site.

What Should Agencies and Publishers Stop Doing in 2026?

Stop treating a strong domain as a vehicle for unrelated content. The tactic the policy targets, often sold as "parasite SEO" or placement on high-authority sites, depends on content ranking because of the host, not because of its own merit. Under Google's published factors, the giveaways are content that looks different, reads worse, has no accountable author, and appears in near-identical form elsewhere.

  • Stop buying placements of distributed content that also runs on other sites.
  • Stop publishing unbranded, unauthored commercial pages that are not linked from your own sections.
  • Stop hosting content far outside your domain's subject purely because it ranks there.
  • Do disclose commercial partnerships clearly and name who is editorially responsible.
  • Do integrate partner content into your design, navigation and editorial standards.

The same principle runs through Google's other 2026 actions. The back button hijacking policy targets tactics that manipulate users, and the February 2026 Discover core update favours expertise judged topic by topic. All three reward content that stands on its own.

What Happens If a Site Receives a Manual Action in 2026?

Google notifies the site in the Manual actions report and the Search Console message center. Google's announcement and policy state that site owners can address the issue or appeal via a reconsideration request, and that eligible sites can, following the reconsideration request, bring disputes to mediation. Mediation is new language in the 2026 update and is worth knowing about if you believe an action was taken in error.

  1. Check the Manual actions report in Search Console for the affected section.
  2. Review that section against Google's four factors: presentation, quality, authorship, duplication.
  3. Integrate, rewrite, attribute or remove content that fails them.
  4. Submit a reconsideration request explaining what changed.
  5. If eligible and you believe the action was wrong, use the mediation route Google describes.

What Are the Common Mistakes in 2026?

  • Reading the EEA change as a softening everywhere. Outside the EEA, including India, actions apply as before.
  • Assuming all third-party content is risky. Google explicitly lists syndication, UGC, editorial work and disclosed advertorials as acceptable.
  • Relying on a single factor. Google says no single factor is necessary or sufficient.
  • Hiding commercial content from navigation. Lack of integration is a named giveaway.
  • Publishing anonymous affiliate pages. Authorship and editorial responsibility are named factors.
  • Buying "high-authority placements". If the content would not rank on its own, that is what the policy describes.

Key Takeaways for 2026

The site reputation policy did not loosen in 2026. Google published clearer criteria and split enforcement by geography, which makes the rules easier to follow and harder to argue with.

  • The policy targets third-party content published mainly to exploit a host site's ranking signals.
  • From 30 August 2026, manual actions apply outside the EEA but not inside it, where sections may rank independently instead.
  • For Indian searchers, effects are unchanged.
  • Google's four factors are presentation, quality, authorship and duplication, none decisive alone.
  • Disclosed advertorials, syndication, UGC and properly treated affiliate links are explicitly acceptable.
  • Reconsideration requests remain, and eligible sites can now pursue mediation.
