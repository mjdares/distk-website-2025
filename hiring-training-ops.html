The Stage Gap

Hiring breaks somewhere between your third person and your thirtieth.

Not because founders are bad at it. Because what worked at three stops working at ten, and nothing has replaced it yet.

01

The signals invert

At five hundred people, a familiar company name on a CV is useful shorthand. At eight people it can mislead. Someone excellent inside a structure may struggle where there is no structure to lean on, and that difference does not show up on paper.

02

Time collapses the process

Founders interview between customer calls. Screens get shortened, notes never get written, follow-up slips by a week. The strongest candidates take another offer while a decision is still pending, and nobody records why.

03

The cost is proportional

A hire who does not work out is a setback at two hundred people. At eight it is an eighth of the company, several months of runway, and the workload lands straight back on the founder who was trying to buy time.

What We Screen For

The best early hire is rarely the most impressive CV in the pile.

This is the part that experience actually teaches. An early-stage role is not a defined job on a defined ladder, so the traits that predict success are not the ones a conventional hiring process is built to detect.

Coachability is the trait that compounds. A capable person who takes feedback well becomes the person you needed within six months. A brilliant person who does not will be the same person in two years, and by then they are senior enough that nobody tells them.

Nurturing & Training

Hiring the person is half of it. The other half is the first ninety days.

Someone joining a five-person company does not walk into an induction programme, a manager with spare bandwidth, or a training budget. They walk straight into the work. Without any structure around that, capable people stall, quietly conclude they are not learning anything, and leave inside a year. The company then reads this as a hiring mistake when it was an onboarding one.

So we design what a larger organisation would provide by default: a ninety-day plan with defined milestones, a weekly check-in cadence built to survive a busy quarter, and a written record of what this person is expected to learn and by when. None of it is elaborate. It just has to exist before day one rather than get invented in week three.

The exchange here runs both ways, and it is worth naming honestly rather than dressing up. A person joining an early team gets responsibility and range they would wait years for in a larger company. The company gets someone who learns the business from the inside and grows into a role that a more senior candidate would have declined at that stage. That trade is genuinely good for both sides, but only while the training half is real. Where it is not, it is simply an underpaid job with a growth story attached.

Your Side of the Line

The obligations that stay with you, including the ones teams miss.

We do not handle documentation, payroll or statutory filing, and we will not imply otherwise. What we will do is tell you what is coming, because these thresholds arrive quietly during a growth phase and a founder counting only full-time staff can cross one without noticing.

Thresholds, wage ceilings and state-level rules change, and several of these interact with your Shops and Establishments registration. Confirm current requirements with your chartered accountant or employment counsel before acting on any of it. Our contribution is narrower and more practical: when a hire is about to move you across one of these lines, we flag it early enough that you can plan for it rather than discover it.

Straight Answers

What founders ask us first.

We have no HR person. Can you handle everything?

No, and it is worth being direct about that. We run role definition, sourcing, initial scrutiny and onboarding design. Employment contracts, payroll, statutory registrations and workplace policies stay with you and your advisors. Most early teams handle that side with a chartered accountant and a reviewed template set, which is usually the right call at this stage and considerably cheaper than the alternative.

How is this different from a recruitment agency?

Recruiters are measured on placements. That is a real service and often exactly the right one, particularly for a well-defined role you need to fill at speed. Our scope sits differently: more of the work happens before sourcing begins, in defining what the role actually is, and after the person joins, in the first ninety days. That is where early-stage hires most often come apart, and it is not what a placement fee is designed to cover.

What kind of roles does this suit?

Roles where the brief will change within six months, which at early stage is most of them. Generalist operations, marketing, sales, and first hires into a function that does not exist yet. Highly specialised senior technical searches are usually better served by a specialist recruiter with a deep network in that field, and we will tell you when we think that is the case rather than take the work.

Do you guarantee a hire will work out?

No. Anyone offering that guarantee is selling you something. What we do instead is make the reasoning visible. You get written arguments for and against each shortlisted person, so the decision rests on documented evidence you can interrogate rather than a gut read after two pleasant conversations. If it goes wrong, you can see where the reasoning failed and correct it next time.

How long does a role take to fill?

It depends on seniority and how defined the role is when we start. The step that most often extends a search is role definition, and it is the step founders most want to skip. A week spent getting the scorecard right routinely saves a month of interviewing people who were never going to fit.

Can you train people we have already hired?

Yes. The ninety-day structure and check-in cadence work just as well for someone who joined six months ago without either. This is a common situation in fast-growing teams, and it is usually far cheaper to fix than to re-hire around a person who is stalling for structural reasons rather than personal ones.
